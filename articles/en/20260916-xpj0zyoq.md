<h2>Body</h2>
<p>I've spent the last month adding features and improving performance in z486_MiSTer , mostly working through</p>
<p>games from the first half of the 1990s. Looking a few years ahead brought me</p>
<p>to another change I wanted to explore: the arrival of 3D graphics cards.</p>
<p>The first one that left a strong impression on me was the Voodoo. The game was Need for Speed II SE . Smooth textures, fog, and the speed of the whole thing</p>
<p>made it feel like a new generation of PC gaming. Could I recreate that on an FPGA</p>
<p>now that the z486 CPU exists?</p>
<p>The result of this detour is zSST , a</p>
<p>SystemVerilog implementation of the 3dfx Voodoo Graphics, or SST-1.</p>
<p>Combined with my z486 CPU and the surrounding PC hardware, it forms z486 XL : a DOS PC with Voodoo graphics</p>
<p>running in the programmable logic of a Xilinx KV260 board. Tomb Raider now runs</p>
<p>with its original 3dfx renderer.</p>
<p>zSST implements most of the central Voodoo features: prepared triangles,</p>
<p>texture filtering and mipmapping, depth and alpha tests, fog, blending,</p>
<p>dithering, framebuffer access, and buffer swaps. It supports both the</p>
<p>fixed-point and floating-point setup interfaces. Hardware game testing is</p>
<p>still concentrated on Tomb Raider; broader compatibility and later Voodoo</p>
<p>generations are work for another day.</p>
<p>The CPU and renderer run at 100 MHz on the KV260 .</p>
<p>That board has enough logic, DSP blocks, on-chip memory, and DDR bandwidth for</p>
<p>the combined design. The DE10-Nano does</p>
<p>not have room for this graphics addition. The KV260 uses its onboard DDR;</p>
<p>there is no external SDRAM module to add.</p>
<p>Fortunately, there is plenty of material to work from. 3dfx released the Glide source</p>
<p>in 1999, before NVIDIA acquired its core graphics assets in December 2000.</p>
<p>The surviving Glide source and SST-1 specification explain how software prepares triangles, configures the pixel pipeline, and</p>
<p>manages textures and framebuffers.</p>
<p>The specification is a behavioral target, rather than a circuit diagram.</p>
<p>It tells what should happen when software writes a register, but leaves</p>
<p>many implementation choices open. 86Box provides useful references for complicated rendering behavior. The earlier MAME Voodoo work is another</p>
<p>part of this preservation history. SpinalVoodoo supplied particularly</p>
<p>useful Glide traces and reference screenshots for testing.</p>
<p>Voodoo Graphics turns triangles into pixels, leaving much of the 3D work to</p>
<p>the host CPU. Its command interface is surprisingly compact: five main</p>
<p>command registers drive the accelerator.</p>
<p>Both triangle commands launch the same rendering pipeline. Other registers</p>
<p>hold coordinates, gradients, and render state, while memory-mapped regions</p>
<p>provide texture uploads and direct framebuffer access. The main drawing</p>
<p>primitive is simply a prepared triangle.</p>
<p>For game developers, Glide presents a friendlier interface:</p>
<p>Before this call, the host CPU transforms the 3D geometry, computes vertex</p>
<p>lighting, clips it, and projects it onto the screen. Glide then prepares the</p>
<p>screen-space triangle and its parameter gradients—the increments used to</p>
<p>interpolate values across its surface—and writes the triangle command to</p>
<p>start rendering. Unlike later GPUs such as the GeForce 256, SST-1 has no</p>
<p>hardware transform-and-lighting engine.</p>
<p>That still leaves plenty of work for the accelerator. The rasterizer finds</p>
<p>which pixel centers lie inside the triangle and interpolates their color,</p>
<p>depth, and texture coordinates. The texture unit fetches and filters texels;</p>
<p>the framebuffer unit combines colors, applies visibility tests and fog,</p>
<p>blends with the existing image, and writes the result.</p>
<p>The original card divides this work between two ASICs: the FBI , or Frame</p>
<p>Buffer Interface, and TREX , the texture mapping unit, usually called the</p>
<p>TMU. At a 50 MHz graphics clock, the advertised peak is one textured,</p>
<p>depth-tested output pixel per clock: 50 million pixels per second.</p>
<p>One pixel per clock does not mean that a pixel finishes in one clock. It means</p>
<p>that different stages can work on different pixels simultaneously: while one</p>
<p>pixel is being textured, an earlier one can be blended and another written</p>
<p>out. Once the pipeline is full, it can ideally accept and finish a pixel</p>
<p>every clock, provided memory keeps up.</p>
<p>That is the appeal of a fixed-function pipeline. A software renderer executes</p>
<p>many instructions for each pixel; dedicated hardware overlaps that work across</p>
<p>a steady stream of pixels. Voodoo brought richly textured 3D games to life at</p>
<p>a fluid 30 FPS or more—a big part of what made it so popular.</p>
<p>Compared with an x86 CPU, the arithmetic path is pleasantly regular. Let's</p>
<p>follow a pixel from its interpolated parameters through texturing and color</p>
<p>operations to the framebuffer, starting with how the numbers are represented.</p>
<p>Floating-point arithmetic is central to modern GPU programming. SST-1 sits at</p>
<p>an interesting transition: software can submit floating-point values, but the</p>
<p>rendering machinery largely operates in fixed point—integers with an implicit</p>
<p>scale factor.</p>
<p>Here 12.4 means twelve bits before the binary point, including the sign,</p>
<p>and four fractional bits. A screen coordinate of 10.5 is therefore stored as</p>
<p>the integer 168: multiply by 16 to encode it, divide by 16 to recover the</p>
<p>value. Those fractional bits let the rasterizer handle vertices between pixel</p>
<p>centers.</p>
<p>The fvertex , fstart , and floating-point gradient registers accept IEEE</p>
<p>single-precision values. SST-1 converts them into its internal fixed-point</p>
<p>representation, and zSST follows that contract. Once the triangle is prepared,</p>
<p>advancing along a scanline mostly means adding a precomputed increment to each</p>
<p>interpolated parameter. Much of the pixel-by-pixel work becomes simple integer</p>
<p>addition.</p>
<p>For perspective-correct texturing, the TMU interpolates S/W, T/W, and 1/W,</p>
<p>then divides the first two by the third to recover texture coordinates. This</p>
<p>keeps a floor or wall texture in perspective as the surface recedes. The TMU</p>
<p>also selects a mip level : a smaller</p>
<p>version of the texture for pixels that cover a larger area of its surface.</p>
<p>This reduces aliasing and shimmering in the distance.</p>
<p>Bilinear filtering then combines four neighboring texels—the pixels of the</p>
<p>texture—around the sample position. First blend the top pair horizontally,</p>
<p>then the bottom pair, and finally blend vertically between those two results.</p>
<p>The fractional position determines the weights, producing a smooth transition</p>
<p>between texel colors instead of an abrupt jump from one to the next.</p>
<p>In zSST, a four-stage front end pipelines the perspective and level-of-detail</p>
<p>calculations. Address generation and cache lookup supply the texels, and two</p>
<p>registered decode stages turn their stored formats into colors for filtering</p>
<p>and texture combining. Palette-based and NCC-encoded textures need different</p>
<p>decoding rules, but ultimately feed the same pixel stream.</p>
<p>Once texture and framebuffer data are available, zSST's FBI pixel path uses</p>
<p>six registered stages:</p>
<p>These stage boundaries are chosen to meet the FPGA's clock target. The SST-1 specification</p>
<p>describes the operations but does not reveal the original ASIC's exact</p>
<p>pipeline registers. Splitting fog lookup from fog application, for example,</p>
<p>keeps a long arithmetic path out of a single clock while retaining the ability</p>
<p>to accept one pixel per clock.</p>
<p>The result is written to the back buffer. A retrace-synchronized buffer swap</p>
<p>then displays the finished image without switching buffers halfway through scanout.</p>
<p>The original Voodoo was a 3D-only add-on, passing the ordinary VGA card's output</p>
<p>through when inactive. z486 XL makes the analogous selection between the PC's</p>
<p>VGA output and zSST's display output inside the FPGA system.</p>
<p>The zSST pixel pipeline proved relatively straightforward to implement,</p>
<p>at least compared with z486's CPU pipelines. Keeping it fed turned out to be</p>
<p>much harder. A bilinear sample needs four texels from separate addresses.</p>
<p>A depth-tested, blended pixel also needs the existing depth and color, followed</p>
<p>by writes of the new values. Performing those accesses one at a time quickly</p>
<p>destroys throughput. I ended up spending more time designing, tuning, and</p>
<p>debugging the memory system than the arithmetic pipeline.</p>
<p>The division of labor is visible on this Diamond Monster 3D. The upper 3dfx</p>
<p>chip is the TMU , the lower one the FBI , each with four EDO RAM chips to</p>
<p>its right. The upper group holds textures; the lower group holds color and</p>
<p>depth/alpha buffers.</p>
<p>FBI and TMU each have a dedicated 64-bit memory path. On the texture side,</p>
<p>four-way interleaving lets the banks read independent addresses, supplying the</p>
<p>four neighbors for bilinear filtering in parallel. The specification (p. 13) promises the same throughput as point sampling, without storing duplicate texels.</p>
<p>But what if two neighboring texels land in the same chip?</p>
<p>The trick is to</p>
<p>distribute texels in a repeating two-dimensional pattern, rather than split</p>
<p>the image into four large regions. Assign a bank to each combination of even</p>
<p>or odd column and row, and the reason becomes clear:</p>
<p>Every 2×2 window contains A, B, C, and D—even the orange window crossing both</p>
<p>horizontal and vertical block boundaries. Two consecutive columns have</p>
<p>opposite parity, as do two consecutive rows. All four combinations occur</p>
<p>exactly once, so each bank supplies one texel with no conflict.</p>
<p>Texture edges and small mip levels need a little more care. SST-1 uses</p>
<p>power-of-two texture dimensions, so wrapping preserves the alternating</p>
<p>pattern for dimensions of two or more. At clamped edges, or in mip levels</p>
<p>only one texel wide or high, some samples reuse the same texel. The central</p>
<p>insight remains: fast bilinear filtering depends on arranging memory so that</p>
<p>the arithmetic receives all its inputs together.</p>
<p>The FBI applies a similar idea to color and depth/alpha memory. Its interleaved</p>
<p>path supports a peak of one rendered pixel per clock, or two pixels per clock</p>
<p>for clears. Working on adjacent pixels together spreads the read/write cost</p>
<p>across a scanline. Fabien Sanglard's two-pixel explanation offers a useful</p>
<p>reconstruction of this behavior, though the exact ASIC bank schedule is not</p>
<p>documented in the programming guide.</p>
<p>At 50 MHz, each 64-bit path has a theoretical bandwidth of 400 MB/s: 800 MB/s</p>
<p>in total, but reserved for different jobs. The TMU cannot borrow idle FBI</p>
<p>bandwidth, or vice versa. These dedicated buses and carefully arranged banks</p>
<p>remind me of the NES- and SNES-era designs I explored in projects such as SNESTang : getting the most out of memory means</p>
<p>designing around exactly when and where each value is needed.</p>
<p>Voodoo's memory layout explains how it kept the pipeline busy, but I cannot</p>
<p>simply transplant that design to the KV260. The board has much more memory</p>
<p>bandwidth, yet no dedicated EDO memory attached to either rendering unit.</p>
<p>Instead, the FPGA accesses shared DDR through the Zynq processing system's</p>
<p>AXI ports. Linux, the FPGA PC, and display scanout all compete for that memory.</p>
<p>The goal is the same—keep the pixel pipeline fed—but the way to achieve it</p>
<p>has to change.</p>
<p>Our KV260 measurements show why bandwidth alone is not enough. A 128-bit port</p>
<p>at 100 MHz has a theoretical bandwidth of 1.6 GB/s. With one request outstanding,</p>
<p>a 4 KiB read reaches 1,370 MiB/s, but a 64-byte read reaches only 189 MiB/s.</p>
<p>The first data typically takes about 280 ns to arrive—roughly 28 clocks at</p>
<p>100 MHz—with occasional much longer waits.</p>
<p>A renderer that waits for each small read before issuing the next will spend</p>
<p>most of its time idle. zSST needs enough independent work in flight to cover</p>
<p>those waits.</p>
<p>Keeping the pipeline fed requires both fewer DDR accesses and less time spent</p>
<p>waiting for them. The first step is caching . Nearby screen pixels often</p>
<p>sample overlapping parts of a texture, so recently fetched texels can be reused</p>
<p>from on-chip RAM. zSST's texture cache holds 8 KiB in 64-byte lines; each fetch</p>
<p>also brings in neighboring texels that subsequent pixels are likely to need.</p>
<p>A cache miss still takes many clocks, but independent texture samples need</p>
<p>not wait for it. zSST keeps up to eight cache-line fetches outstanding, using</p>
<p>a replay queue to park samples with missing data and retry them when it</p>
<p>arrives. Meanwhile, samples whose texels are already cached can proceed.</p>
<p>Prefetching gets a head start on future reads.</p>
<p>Now a later cache hit can finish before an earlier miss. A 64-entry reorder buffer , or ROB, collects those results and releases them in their</p>
<p>original order. The principle is familiar from CPUs: do useful work during a</p>
<p>long wait, then restore order before passing the results downstream.</p>
<p>The framebuffer side uses separate 4 KiB color and depth/alpha read caches,</p>
<p>while write combiners pack neighboring 16-bit updates into 128-bit requests.</p>
<p>Here, ordering matters: blending or depth testing may need a value that an</p>
<p>earlier pixel has changed but not yet written to DDR. Forwarding supplies the</p>
<p>pending value directly. Framebuffer updates take effect in order, and state</p>
<p>changes that require completed work wait for it to drain. Memory requests can</p>
<p>overlap, but later pixels must still see the effects of earlier ones.</p>
<p>FBI and TMU share the renderer's 128-bit AXI port, HP2. The PC uses HP0 and</p>
<p>display scanout uses HP3, keeping their request queues separate even though</p>
<p>all three ultimately share DDR.</p>
<p>I measure the renderer separately from the complete PC. The simulation</p>
<p>benchmark sends commands through zSST's front end and exercises the TMU, FBI,</p>
<p>shared arbiter, and a DDR timing model. Read data arrives after at least 26</p>
<p>clocks, with deterministic variation and occasional longer delays; writes are</p>
<p>also rate-limited. The full-renderer tests allow 32 outstanding reads.</p>
<p>At 100 MHz, zSST reaches 78.5 million pixels per second (MPix/s) for textured</p>
<p>triangles, and 72.8 MPix/s with depth testing and blending. Voodoo 1's published</p>
<p>estimates at its native 50 MHz are 43 and 37 MPix/s for comparable feature</p>
<p>sets. This is not an apples-to-apples benchmark: the triangle workloads differ,</p>
<p>and the original estimates also include fog, mipmapping, and Gouraud shading.</p>
<p>I do not have a Voodoo 1 to run the same test on both. The comparison shows</p>
<p>the approximate fill-rate range, not a measured speedup over the original card.</p>
<p>High fill rates do not automatically translate into high game FPS. On the</p>
<p>board, Tomb Raider Level 2 produced 237 displayed buffer swaps in about 20</p>
<p>seconds—roughly 12 per second, measured from swaps rather than an engine FPS</p>
<p>counter. Preliminary measurements point to a CPU bottleneck: it still has to</p>
<p>run the game, prepare geometry, and submit commands. Shared DDR contention</p>
<p>may also contribute. There is plenty left to optimize in the complete machine.</p>
<p>For non-Voodoo games, the current 100 MHz z486 XL runs maximum-detail Doom at</p>
<p>38.5 FPS and Quake 1.06 at 8.1 FPS. That is roughly 20% faster than the 85 MHz</p>
<p>DE10-Nano build—about 23% for Doom and 19% for Quake. A 512 KiB write-back L2</p>
<p>cache in UltraRAM helps the CPU make better use of DDR.</p>
<p>In the integrated XCK26 build, zSST accounts for about 29,500 LUTs, 28,100</p>
<p>flip-flops, 14 RAMB36 blocks, 8 RAMB18 blocks, and 97 DSP slices. The combined</p>
<p>PC and graphics design meets timing at 100 MHz.</p>
<p>The rewarding part is seeing original Glide software drive hardware I built</p>
<p>in RTL. I expected the rendering arithmetic to be the hard part; getting data</p>
<p>to it efficiently took more work. Voodoo's carefully interleaved EDO and</p>
<p>zSST's caches and queues solve the same problem under very different</p>
<p>constraints: a fast pixel pipeline is only useful when it has something to do.</p>
<p>Both zSST and z486 XL are available open source.</p>
<p>If you already have a KV260, the z486 XL SD image provides the Linux support and application needed to launch your own DOS disk</p>
<p>images.</p>
